Check an email’s sender.

See whether an email’s DKIM signature supports the email address in its From field.

A valid DKIM signature is evidence that the sender’s email service processed and sent the message as-is. It is very strong evidence that someone with access to that email account sent it.

The verification is done locally, in this browser tab. Your email will not be transmitted over the internet.

The signature will not validate unless you have exactly the email contents which were sent. So, for instance, you can't use this with mail received by Proton Mail, because it changes the .eml files.

Use “Download original” or “Save as” in your mail app. Maximum 20 MiB.

Include all headers and the body. Copying, rich-text editors, or a different text encoding can break a signature; uploading preserves the original bytes.

When a key is not in the local collection, ask Google Public DNS for it. Google sees the signing domain, key name, and your IP address—not the email contents. Uncheck for no network requests while verifying. Current DNS may no longer contain keys used for older emails.

Leave blank to use now. Only enter a time you independently know: a message’s own Date field is not proof of when it arrived.

For an uncovered domain, you may supply one public DNS TXT record. A match against a supplied key does not authenticate its owner.

A valid signature does not prove who wrote the email, whether its claims are true, or that every header was signed. This tool checks DKIM, not SPF, DMARC, or ARC. Archived keys can verify old signatures after removal from DNS; this is not a live mail-delivery verdict.

No email uploads, analytics, or third-party scripts. The page loads its bundled catalogue. When enabled, missing-key lookups send only DNS names to Google Public DNS; cryptographic checks stay local. Following a citation opens its source.
